03–05 OCT

Arrival & preparation

The international team began arriving in Angola, with introductions, museum visits and final logistical preparations ahead of the field campaign.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

A campaign like this begins long before the first fossil is exposed: with people coming together, equipment being prepared and plans being coordinated.

06 OCT

The field season begins

The team set off for Bentiaba and the first field activities began. Early messages also shared fossil-related material, including a tooth and a mark interpreted in discussion as a possible bite mark. These observations should remain provisional pending scientific confirmation.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

The first days are about settling into the field rhythm—and learning to look closely. Even a small mark or isolated tooth can raise questions worth investigating.

07–09 OCT

The journey south

Teams travelled by road and air toward the field site. Messages describe difficult stretches of road, changing travel plans and the coordination required to move people and equipment to Bentiaba.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

Field science depends on logistics as much as discovery: getting people, supplies and equipment safely to a remote site is part of the expedition story.

10 OCT

Building the field station

The teams arrived at camp and began setting up. Messages mention the practical work of securing water, setting up tents and working on electricity. Road conditions were mixed, with some damaged stretches and a section where the road gave way to off-road travel.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

Before the scientific work can get going, a field station has to work. Water, shelter, power and teamwork are essential infrastructure for research in a remote landscape.

11 OCT

Reading the evidence

A specimen discussion focused on a mosasaur caudal vertebra and its haemal arch. The team was reminded to include a millimetre/centimetre scale in specimen photographs so images can also support scientific comparison.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

A fossil photograph is more useful when it records not only what a specimen looks like, but also its size and context. Documentation is part of the science.

12 OCT

A pterosaur bone—and fieldwork beyond the main camp

A pterosaur bone was shared from the previous day’s work. The wider team was also coordinating travel between field locations, including a return from Baía dos Tigres where connectivity had been unavailable.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

The field record includes both individual finds and the movements that connect different locations, teams and stages of work.

13 OCT

A new jaw near the beach

Arthur reported a new jaw from the same stratigraphic level as material described in the chat as “lindesaurus”, located closer to the beach. Other fragments prompted discussion about possible identification, with scientists noting that further views or comparisons would be needed. A separate fragment was tentatively considered to be a sea-turtle rib or a mosasaur haemal spine.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

Identification is often a process, not an instant answer. Researchers compare shapes, context and other specimens before deciding what a fragment can reliably tell us.

14 OCT

Back in Bentiaba

The group shared a large batch of photographs and videos from the field. A mark on a rock prompted a question about whether it could be a shark tooth; another message raised “Globidens?” as a possible identification. Both remain questions rather than confirmed identifications in the available chat.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

The expedition is a collective act of observation: a detail noticed by one person can become a question for the wider team.

15 OCT

A halisaurine enters the species list

Mike Polcyn identified a specimen discussed in the chat as a halisaurine and noted that the Angolan taxon represents a new species in the team’s list to complete. Jonas later clarified that only fragmentary jaw material was visible ex situ and that it was too brittle to collect. This is a significant scientific lead, but public wording should be checked with the researchers before publication.

POSTING ANGLE / DRAFT

One of the most important outcomes of fieldwork can be a new research lead—even when a fragile specimen cannot be collected. The evidence, its limits and the next questions all matter.

Editorial notes for communications

  • Distinguish confirmed identifications from hypotheses and questions raised in the group.
  • For the 15 October halisaurine discussion, confirm the exact taxonomic wording and whether the team is comfortable describing it publicly as a new species/taxon.
  • For specimen images, preserve scale and context wherever available; avoid implying that every fragment was collected.
  • The WhatsApp thread gives little detail on the computer-vision/dentition project so far. Add a dedicated entry when the student shares the workflow, aims, methods and limitations.
  • Continue updating this diary after the expedition. Retrospective notes and images are welcome; no daily content production is required from the field team.
